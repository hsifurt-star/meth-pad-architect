# Evidence: personal estimates versus population life-years

## What predictive models support

A 2024 systematic review found 41 opioid-related studies that developed more than 160 models for opioid overdose, opioid use disorder, or persistent opioid use. Predictors and performance varied; reported discrimination ranged from 0.507 to 0.959, risk of bias was predominantly high, and most validation was internal. These are outcome-specific models, not a validated calculator for arbitrary drugs, doses, routes, age, duration, and co-use histories.[1]

WHO ASSIST and NIDA TAPS are substance-specific screening tools, not personal life-expectancy calculators. NIDA says TAPS should be used under medical-provider supervision and is not for self-diagnosis.[2][3]

## What population life tables support

A New South Wales cohort study followed 47,197 people prescribed opioid agonist treatment from 2001 to 2018. It estimated average life expectancy in that cohort at approximately 15 years below the general population.[4] This is a group estimate for that place, treatment population, and period—not a forecast for an individual or other substances.

A 2024 U.S. study used age-specific opioid-overdose deaths and cause-eliminated life tables. It estimated a 0.67-year reduction in U.S. life expectancy at birth attributable to opioid-overdose mortality in 2022, 3.082 million total years of life lost, and 37.7 years lost per opioid-related death.[5] Those population mortality measures are not the remaining lifespan or years lost by a living person who uses drugs.

## Interface implication

Keep the individual “years of life lost” result as **not estimable from this history**. A compact, optional source note may show the NSW cohort estimate with its population limits; never apply it to a person, a dose, or another drug.
